There are four main tokenization schemes you'll encounter, each solving the same core tradeoff differently: **vocabulary size vs. sequence length vs. handling of rare/unseen words.**

**1. Word-level tokenization** Splits text on whitespace/punctuation — each word is a token. "unbelievable" stays as one token.

- _Problem_: huge vocabulary (every word form needed — "run," "runs," "running" are all separate entries). Any word not seen during training becomes `<UNK>` (out-of-vocabulary), losing information entirely.
- _Interview angle_: this is the "naive baseline" — know it mainly to explain why it was abandoned for OOV handling and vocab explosion.

**2. Character-level tokenization** Splits into individual characters. "cats" → ["c","a","t","s"].

- _Pro_: tiny vocabulary (just the alphabet + symbols), zero OOV problem — any string can be represented.
- _Con_: sequences become very long, and each character carries little meaning on its own, forcing the model to work much harder to learn structure. Compute cost scales with sequence length (attention is O(n²)), so this is expensive.
- _Interview angle_: good for showing you understand the length/vocab tradeoff.

**3. Subword tokenization (the modern default)** Splits into pieces smaller than words but larger than characters — common words stay whole, rare words get broken into meaningful chunks. "unbelievable" → ["un", "believ", "able"]. This balances vocabulary size against sequence length, and handles rare/unseen words gracefully (never a true OOV, since you can always fall back to smaller pieces).

There are several algorithms under this umbrella — this is where interviewers usually probe:

- **BPE (Byte-Pair Encoding)** — used in GPT models. Starts with individual characters as the vocabulary, then iteratively merges the _most frequent adjacent pair_ of tokens into a new single token, repeating until a target vocab size is reached. It's a greedy, frequency-driven merge process, done once at training time.
- **WordPiece** — used in BERT. Very similar to BPE (also builds vocab via merges), but instead of choosing the most _frequent_ pair to merge, it chooses the pair that most increases the _likelihood_ of the training data under a language-model objective. Marks subword continuations with `##` (e.g., "believable" → ["believ", "##able"]).
- **Unigram (SentencePiece's default)** — works in the opposite direction: starts with a large vocabulary and iteratively _removes_ tokens that least hurt overall likelihood, until reaching the target size. Also probabilistic — it can generate multiple possible tokenizations for the same string and pick the most likely one.
- **SentencePiece** — technically a tokenization _framework_, not an algorithm itself. It implements both BPE and Unigram, but its key feature is that it treats text as a raw stream of bytes/characters (including whitespace as a symbol, often "▁") — meaning no pre-tokenization by whitespace is required. This makes it language-agnostic, working well for languages like Japanese or Chinese where words aren't whitespace-separated.
- **Byte-level BPE** — a variant used in GPT-2/GPT-3+ that operates on raw UTF-8 bytes rather than Unicode characters as the base unit. This guarantees a fixed, small base vocabulary (256 possible byte values) and means _any_ input, including emojis or unseen scripts, can always be tokenized — there is never a true "unknown character."

**Quick comparison table**

|Scheme|Vocab size|Sequence length|OOV handling|
|---|---|---|---|
|Word-level|Huge|Short|Poor (`<UNK>`)|
|Character-level|Tiny|Very long|Perfect but inefficient|
|Subword (BPE/WordPiece/Unigram)|Medium (tuned)|Medium|Good — falls back to smaller pieces|

**Likely interview follow-ups to be ready for:**

- _"Why does GPT use BPE but BERT use WordPiece?"_ — both are subword methods with similar goals; the difference is the merge criterion (frequency vs. likelihood), and it's mostly a design choice inherited by each model family rather than a fundamental capability gap.
- _"How does tokenization affect model performance on math or code?"_ — subword tokenizers often split numbers inconsistently (e.g., "1234" might become ["12","34"] or ["1","234"]), which is a known cause of arithmetic errors in LLMs — a good example of tokenization choices leaking into model behavior downstream.
- _"What determines vocab size?"_ — a hyperparameter tuned during tokenizer training; bigger vocab = shorter sequences but more parameters in the embedding/output layers, and rarer tokens are seen fewer times during training.
